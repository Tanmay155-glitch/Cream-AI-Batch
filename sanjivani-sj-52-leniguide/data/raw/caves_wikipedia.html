Ajanta Caves

Note: This is fallback content for reference purposes.

The Ajanta Caves are approximately 30 rock-cut Buddhist cave monuments which date from the 2nd century BCE to about 480 CE in the Aurangabad district of Maharashtra state in India.

History

The caves were built in two phases: the earlier Hinayana phase (2nd-1st century BCE) and the later Mahayana phase (5th century CE). The site was abandoned and forgotten until 1819 when British officer John Smith rediscovered it during a hunting expedition.

Architecture and Art

The caves include both chaitya halls (prayer halls) and viharas (monasteries). The paintings and sculptures at Ajanta are considered masterpieces of Buddhist religious art that influenced subsequent Indian art.

Famous Features

  • Padmapani Bodhisattva: One of the most iconic murals from Cave 1
  • Jataka Tales: Stories of Buddha's previous lives depicted in paintings
  • Architectural Innovation: Advanced rock-cutting and painting techniques
  • Color Preservation: Natural pigments still visible after 1500+ years

Conservation

The Archaeological Survey of India maintains the site. Photography with flash is prohibited to preserve the ancient paintings. Climate control measures are in place in several caves.
